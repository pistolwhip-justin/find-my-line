# Find-My-Line — Phased Marketing, User Testing, and Growth Plan

This document makes user acquisition part of product development rather than something postponed until launch.

## 1. Why marketing starts before launch

Find-My-Line is a route-discovery system whose quality depends heavily on real riders using it in real places. Growing the tester base during development serves several purposes at once:

- Finds usability problems before they become architectural problems.
- Tests whether generated routes are actually useful to riders.
- Produces real-world route validation and failure reports.
- Builds the community that will later provide route/segment feedback.
- Creates authentic examples, screenshots, GPX demonstrations and ride stories.
- Gives the project a growing group of people who can test each major capability as it arrives.
- Creates a measurable feedback loop between development and real-world use.

Marketing therefore means **recruiting the right riders, not maximizing downloads**.

The primary early audience is bikepackers, gravel riders, adventure cyclists and experienced off-road cyclists who are willing to test unfamiliar routes and provide useful feedback.

## 2. Growth philosophy

### Quality before scale

Do not recruit hundreds of people before the product can support them. Each phase has a target tester population and a quality gate.

The target is a **cumulative active tester population**, not merely the number of people who ever downloaded an APK.

A tester counts as active when they have used the current build/version for a meaningful planning or route-testing task within the current test period.

### Recruit in waves

Each phase should use several small recruitment waves rather than one large launch:

1. Recruit a small cohort.
2. Observe usage.
3. Fix the largest problems.
4. Recruit the next cohort.
5. Compare results against the previous cohort.
6. Increase the population only when the product can absorb it.

### Avoid vanity metrics

Track:

- testers recruited
- testers activated
- testers who generated at least one route
- routes generated
- routes selected/exported
- routes actually ridden
- post-ride reports received
- route/segment problems discovered
- useful feedback per active tester
- retention into the next test version
- referral rate
- acquisition source
- cost per activated tester
- percentage of testers who would use the planner again

Do **not** treat raw downloads, social followers, impressions or website visits as product success by themselves.

## 3. Target tester growth by phase

These are planning targets, not promises. The numbers should be adjusted when actual recruitment or product quality data shows that a phase needs more or fewer people.

| Phase | Primary objective | Target active testers | Approx. cumulative testers | Main acquisition approach |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Phase 0 | Recruit design partners / route experts | 0–10 | 0–10 | Personal outreach and trusted cycling contacts |
| Phase 1 | Prove route generator with real riders | 10–25 | 10–25 | Direct outreach, bikepacking communities, local riders |
| Phase 2 | Validate route intelligence | 25–75 | 35–100 | Community posts, referrals, ride examples, targeted outreach |
| Phase 3 | Test regional/offline data system | 50–150 | 85–250 | Regional cohorts, bikepacking groups, geographic expansion |
| Phase 4 | Validate local AI planning | 100–300 | 185–550 | Existing testers + AI/offline enthusiasts + referrals |
| Phase 5 | Build community feedback loop | 250–750 | 435–1,300 | Rider referrals, route stories, ambassador program, communities |
| Phase 6 | Test interoperability | 500–1,500 | 935–2,800 | Navigation-tool users, GPX/FIT users, content and referrals |
| Phase 7 | Validate advanced discovery | 1,000–3,000 | 1,935–5,800 | Adventure riders, route explorers, regional ambassadors |
| Phase 8 | Broader ecosystem adoption | 3,000–10,000+ | 4,935–15,800+ | Organic content, referrals, partnerships and ecosystem integrations |

These figures intentionally overlap because users should carry forward between phases. A smaller number of highly engaged riders is more valuable than a large pool of passive downloads.

## 4. User acquisition channels

### A. Founding rider / design-partner outreach

Use direct personal outreach first.

Target:
- experienced bikepackers
- gravel riders who regularly create their own routes
- riders who already use GPX-based planning
- riders who explore public lands and unpaved corridors
- people who routinely complain that normal route planners produce boring or inappropriate lines

Ask for testing help rather than selling a finished product.

Goal: obtain detailed feedback and real route examples from people who understand the problem.

### B. Bikepacking communities

Recruit through communities where route discovery is already a genuine topic. Examples include relevant Reddit communities, cycling forums, local cycling groups, bikepacking groups and regional adventure-riding communities.

Rules:
- Follow each community's self-promotion rules.
- Never spam repeated promotional posts.
- Lead with useful route-planning information or a real experiment.
- Be transparent that the project is experimental.
- Ask for testers when there is a specific capability to test.
- Share results back with the community when appropriate.

### C. Regional rider recruitment

As regional datasets mature, recruit riders specifically from those regions.

Example:
- Build Appalachian test region.
- Recruit 20–50 riders who know that region.
- Ask them to compare generated lines with their knowledge.
- Record surface/access/closure/technicality errors.
- Fix the data/routing model.
- Publish a useful case study.

This produces much stronger validation than generic worldwide downloads.

### D. Referral loop

After a tester successfully uses Find-My-Line, make it easy to invite another rider.

The referral message should emphasize the specific capability that was useful, not generic "download my app" language.

Potential trigger:
> "Find-My-Line found me a route with 72% unpaved riding that I wouldn't have connected manually. Want to test it?"

Do not introduce paid referral incentives until organic referral behavior is understood.

### E. Ride reports and route stories

Real routes become marketing assets only after they are validated.

Potential content:
- "Find-My-Line found this connection"
- route comparison before/after
- pavement reduction examples
- difficult access/closure discoveries
- first-documented-rider stories where verification rules are satisfied
- post-ride lessons
- regional route-discovery reports
- screenshots of candidate comparisons
- GPX planning walkthroughs

The content should demonstrate what the system does rather than making unsupported claims that it is the best route planner.

### F. Search-friendly website content

Build a library of useful pages around real planning questions rather than generic SEO filler.

Examples:
- bikepacking route planning for [region]
- gravel route planning for [region]
- how to find low-pavement bikepacking routes
- public-land bikepacking route considerations
- comparing route surfaces
- route-planning mistakes and how to avoid them

Where appropriate, pages can show anonymized route examples and explain the underlying data sources.

### G. Ambassadors / regional testers

Begin a lightweight ambassador program only after Phase 5 has enough product stability to support it.

An ambassador is primarily a **field tester and local expert**, not an influencer hired to advertise the app.

Good ambassadors:
- know their local roads/trails
- ride regularly
- can document route conditions
- are willing to report bad routes as well as good ones
- understand that negative findings are valuable

Start with a small number of regions rather than attempting worldwide coverage.

### H. Partnerships

Later opportunities may include:
- bikepacking clubs
- cycling organizations
- local riding groups
- regional trail/public-land communities
- route-planning communities
- bike shops that serve adventure riders
- GPS/navigation communities

Partnerships should come after Find-My-Line has a demonstrable capability worth sharing.

## 5. Phase-by-phase marketing program

### Phase 0 — Design partners

**Target:** 0–10 active testers.

Recruit a small founding group before Phase 1 is complete.

Profile:
- route planners
- experienced bikepackers
- gravel/off-road riders
- people willing to test rough software

Activities:
- personal invitations
- one-on-one interviews
- collect examples of routes they wish existing tools could find
- establish baseline planning workflows
- create a list of representative real-world route problems

Success condition:
The project has a small group of riders who can repeatedly test builds and provide concrete route cases.

### Phase 1 — Closed alpha

**Target:** 10–25 active testers.

Keep the cohort intentionally small.

Recruitment:
- founding testers
- local cycling contacts
- selected bikepacking/gravel communities
- direct outreach to riders who build their own GPX routes

Testing focus:
- can people understand the product?
- are candidate routes genuinely different?
- do riders understand the statistics?
- can GPX export be used successfully?
- where does the generated route fail in the real world?

Marketing content:
- first route-discovery demonstrations
- before/after route comparisons
- screenshots and short videos
- documented failures and fixes

Do not advertise this as a polished public launch.

### Phase 2 — Intelligent-route beta

**Target:** 25–75 active testers; approximately 35–100 cumulative.

Recruit regionally and by riding style.

Activities:
- community recruitment posts
- referral invitations from Phase 1
- route comparison challenges
- regional test rides
- publish validated examples

Testing focus:
- surface accuracy
- access/legal warnings
- elevation
- traffic/road class
- whether alternative routes represent meaningful choices

Growth gate:
Increase recruitment only when the system can absorb the feedback without drowning development in support requests.

### Phase 3 — Regional/offline beta

**Target:** 50–150 active testers; approximately 85–250 cumulative.

Recruit testers in the first supported regions.

Activities:
- regional test groups
- local cycling clubs/groups
- regional route case studies
- offline-planning challenges
- recruit riders who routinely travel outside cellular coverage

Testing focus:
- regional package installation
- offline planning
- incremental updates
- data accuracy by region
- source conflicts and stale information

The marketing story becomes increasingly concrete: **"Tell us what is wrong with this route in your backyard."**

### Phase 4 — Offline AI beta

**Target:** 100–300 active testers; approximately 185–550 cumulative.

Primary recruitment sources:
- existing testers
- offline-first Android users
- AI/on-device AI enthusiasts
- bikepacking riders who frequently plan without connectivity
- referrals

Testing focus:
- natural-language preference interpretation
- ambiguity handling
- structured constraints
- route comparison explanations
- offline reliability
- model performance on real phones

Content opportunities:
- "Say what kind of ride you want" demonstrations
- prompt-to-route examples
- model-size/performance comparisons
- offline planning demonstrations

Do not market the AI as knowing the map. The routing/data system remains the source of route geometry and geographic facts.

### Phase 5 — Community intelligence beta

**Target:** 250–750 active testers; approximately 435–1,300 cumulative.

This is the first phase where a deliberate community growth loop becomes a major development input.

Recruitment:
- referrals
- regional ambassadors
- bikepacking communities
- post-ride invitations
- validated route-story content
- cycling clubs/groups

Activities:
- post-ride feedback campaigns
- "ride this line and tell us what happened" testing
- regional ambassador cohorts
- route-condition reporting
- publish anonymized aggregated findings

Testing focus:
- usefulness of section-level feedback
- privacy controls
- contribution quality
- whether repeated reports improve route selection
- avoiding popularity bias

### Phase 6 — Interoperability expansion

**Target:** 500–1,500 active testers; approximately 935–2,800 cumulative.

Recruit riders based on the navigation/GPS tools they already use.

Activities:
- GPX/FIT workflows
- device-specific testing groups
- integration walkthrough content
- direct user referrals
- navigation-community outreach

Testing focus:
- export correctness
- handoff/import reliability
- reduced manual steps
- compatibility across supported tools/devices

Marketing message:
**Find-My-Line discovers the route; the rider's existing navigation system follows it.**

### Phase 7 — Advanced discovery

**Target:** 1,000–3,000 active testers; approximately 1,935–5,800 cumulative.

Recruit high-value exploratory riders:
- bikepackers who frequently create custom routes
- long-distance riders
- gravel explorers
- regional route experts
- riders who enjoy discovering connections

Activities:
- regional discovery challenges
- "find a connection" competitions/challenges without requiring prizes
- field-verified route stories
- regional ambassador expansion
- publish discovery case studies

Testing focus:
- genuinely novel connections
- false discoveries
- rideability
- access/legal accuracy
- novelty versus unfamiliarity
- usefulness of bailout/resupply/remoteness information

### Phase 8 — Ecosystem growth

**Target:** 3,000–10,000+ active testers; approximately 4,935–15,800+ cumulative.

At this point growth can become broader because the underlying product, data system and community feedback loop have already been tested.

Acquisition mix:
- organic search/content
- referrals
- regional ambassadors
- cycling communities
- partnerships
- compatible navigation/GPS integrations
- earned media/community coverage

The objective is not simply to maximize installs. It is to establish Find-My-Line as a recognizable route-discovery layer that remains useful independently of any single navigation provider.

## 6. Content engine

Every development phase should deliberately create useful public-facing material.

### Content pipeline

```text
Real rider question
      ↓
Find-My-Line experiment
      ↓
Generated route candidates
      ↓
Real-world test
      ↓
Result / failure / correction
      ↓
Anonymized case study
      ↓
Website article / social post / community discussion
      ↓
New testers
      ↓
More route evidence
```

This makes development itself the source of marketing content.

### Content quality rule

Never manufacture success stories. Failed routes, corrected data and lessons learned can be valuable content when presented honestly.

## 7. Tester onboarding

Every cohort should receive a short onboarding flow:

1. What Find-My-Line does.
2. What it does not do.
3. How to test a route.
4. What information is useful when reporting a problem.
5. How to export/use the GPX.
6. How privacy works.
7. Where to submit feedback.

The goal is to make feedback structured enough that development can act on it.

## 8. Feedback instrumentation

For every testing cohort, collect a minimal event vocabulary rather than a huge analytics system.

Recommended events:

- app_opened
- route_request_started
- route_generated
- candidate_viewed
- candidate_selected
- gpx_exported
- route_imported_elsewhere where detectable/voluntary
- ride_started where implemented
- ride_completed where implemented
- feedback_submitted
- route_problem_reported
- referral_started
- referral_completed

Do not collect precise location histories or other sensitive information unless the feature genuinely requires it and the rider has knowingly consented.

## 9. Acquisition experiments

Do not assume one marketing channel will work. Run small measurable experiments.

For each experiment record:

- channel
- audience
- message
- landing page/version
- number reached
- signups
- activated testers
- routes generated
- retained testers
- feedback submissions
- cost
- time spent
- lessons learned

Example experiments:

- Regional Reddit/community post vs. direct invitation.
- Route case study vs. feature announcement.
- "Less pavement" demonstration vs. generic product description.
- Referral invitation vs. open signup.
- Local cycling-club outreach vs. online community outreach.

The winning channel should be determined from measured activation and retention, not follower or impression counts.

## 10. Marketing budget philosophy

Early phases should be **organic-first and cash-light**.

### Phase 0–2
Target paid marketing budget: **$0**.

Use direct outreach, communities, local riders and useful content.

### Phase 3–5
Target paid marketing budget: **$0–$100/month**, and only spend if a specific experiment has a measurable hypothesis.

Potential legitimate expenses may include a domain, simple hosting, printing for a local event, or small testing/field expenses. Do not buy large ad campaigns.

### Phase 6–7
Target: **$0–$250/month** initially, still favoring organic acquisition, referrals and ambassadors.

### Phase 8
A larger marketing budget can be considered only after retention, route usefulness and referral behavior demonstrate that additional traffic is valuable.

## 11. Growth gates

Growth should be treated as a resource allocation decision.

### Do not increase recruitment when:
- route failures are not being triaged
- support/feedback is accumulating faster than it can be processed
- users cannot understand the product
- privacy/data issues remain unresolved
- regional data is unreliable
- the team is learning nothing from additional users

### Increase recruitment when:
- the current cohort is generating actionable feedback
- major usability problems are being fixed quickly
- route validation is producing useful evidence
- onboarding is repeatable
- support burden per user is falling
- the product has a clearer story to tell

## 12. Core growth KPI dashboard

Maintain a simple dashboard by phase:

| KPI | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Active testers | Actual current testing capacity |
| Activation rate | Whether recruitment produces usable testers |
| Routes generated/tester | Depth of product use |
| Routes actually ridden | Real-world validation |
| Feedback submissions/tester | Learning rate |
| Route issue rate | Product/data quality |
| Repeat-use rate | Whether riders find continuing value |
| Referral rate | Organic product-driven growth |
| Acquisition source | Identifies useful channels |
| Cost per activated tester | Prevents wasteful acquisition |
| Time-to-feedback | Measures development loop speed |
| Verified route examples | Content + evidence asset |

## 13. Overall principle

**Every phase should leave Find-My-Line with more than code.**

It should leave the project with:

- more validated route cases
- more knowledgeable riders
- more real-world evidence
- more useful content
- more precise understanding of the target user
- more confidence in the data
- and, when appropriate, a larger group ready to test the next phase.

The growth program exists to strengthen the product, not to make growth itself the product.
